Most of the time I am talking an agent through a decision, the back-and-forth is disposable. We weigh a couple of options, one of them is obviously fine, we take it, and the reasoning is worth no more than a clear commit message. Every so often, though, I notice partway through that the thing we are settling is going to outlive the code that implements it, that someone (quite possibly me) will stand in front of this choice in a year and wonder what on earth we were thinking. That is the moment a decision wants to become a record.

An architecture decision record, an ADR, is a short document that captures one such choice: the problem, the options we actually weighed, what we picked, and why. It is a record of the decision, not of the code. I have written about the other durable things a session leaves behind, the skills and scripts and instruction files in [How I Drive Agentic Software Engineering](/notes/how-i-drive-agentic-software-engineering), and an ADR is another of those, a different flavour. The judgement is in knowing when one is warranted, and then in getting the agent to write a good one.

## When a decision earns a record

I keep a rough test for architectural significance, and it is the thing I reach for when I am unsure. A decision is worth recording when it affects the structure of a system, its non-functional characteristics, its dependencies, its interfaces, or the techniques used to build it. Sharpening that further, I record a choice that has wide effect, that sits on a genuine trade-off point, that is strict or constraining or non-negotiable, that breaks an assumption someone might reasonably hold, or that was difficult to arrive at. There is a good [architectural significance test](https://medium.com/olzzio/architectural-significance-test-9ff17a9b4490) that frames it through business value and risk, stakeholder concerns, external dependencies, cross-cutting effect, whether it is the first of its kind, and whether the area has caused trouble before. None of this is a checklist I run mechanically, more a set of instincts I have collected.

The harder half is knowing when *not* to write one, and here I lean the other way deliberately. It has never been easier to generate a wall of Markdown, and a repository can drown in earnest documents that nobody reads. So an ADR has a bar to clear before I let it exist. The realisation almost always arrives mid-conversation, while the agent and I are laying out the pros and cons of something, and it is the weight of the impact, or the sheer non-obviousness of the choice, that tips it over. If I find myself wanting to preserve the *thinking* and not just the outcome, that is the signal.

## Getting a good one out of an agent

Once I have decided a choice deserves a record, the writing itself is mostly the agent's job, and the quality turns on a few habits.

The first is that I let the agent discover how the project writes ADRs rather than spelling it out. The Openfire project keeps its records under [`doc/adr`](https://github.com/igniterealtime/Openfire/tree/main/doc/adr) with a README that lays out the house structure, and an agent dropped into that repository will read the existing records and match them without my saying a word. The conventions propagate because they are written down where the work happens.

The second is that an ADR should refine the conversation, not transcribe it. The discussion that produced the decision is rambling and repetitive, as good discussions are, and the record needs to be the concise, clarified version of it, with the dead ends pruned and the live options laid out fairly. What I care about is that the rejected options survive into the document. An ADR that lists only the path we took has thrown away its reason for existing, because the value sits in seeing what we declined and why. The Openfire record on [migrating Blowfish key derivation to PBKDF2](https://github.com/igniterealtime/Openfire/blob/main/doc/adr/004-manual-blowfish-pbkdf2-migration.md) is the standard I have in mind: four options, each with its honest upsides and downsides, including one that turns out to be architecturally impossible because of a bootstrap circular dependency, and the reasoning is laid out so plainly that the path to the conclusion is easy to follow. A reader who disagrees with the outcome can at least see exactly where they part company.

Steering it that way is easier than it used to be. I once kept a section in my global `CLAUDE.md` telling agents how to write an ADR, and I pruned it out as the models improved, because they now carry a perfectly good sense of the form in their training. That is the curation habit from [Tending the Context](/notes/tending-the-context) in miniature, taking an instruction back out once it has stopped justifying the space it occupies. My role in the writing is the one I keep across all of this work: the trade-off decisions are mine to make, so the agent surfaces the options and the costs and I steer, and the record captures the call I made. The old failure mode, where an agent would cram implementation detail into a document that is meant to be about the decision, has largely faded with better models, and I find I run my ADR-review skill over the result far less often than I once did.

## What the record gives later

The reason I bother is that the reasoning, written down, goes on being useful long after the session that produced it.

Agents lean on ADRs heavily, more than I expected when I started writing them. Drop a fresh agent into a system and the records are among the quickest ways for it to absorb not just the shape of the architecture but the forces that produced it, which is exactly the context it needs before it starts changing things. A record that explains why a constraint exists is a record that stops a well-meaning agent from cheerfully undoing it.

People benefit in the same way, and the effect is sharpest on open work. Openfire is more asynchronous than my closed projects, with contributors who are rarely in the same conversation at the same time, and the detailed rationale lets someone follow the route we took to a decision and learn from it. On a closed team that understanding might have lived in a video call and then evaporated the moment the call ended, lost to everyone who joins later. And the someone who joins later is often me, six months on, staring at a line of code that looks wrong until the record reminds me which problem it was quietly solving.

<p class="key-line">An ADR is the reasoning made durable, handed to whoever, or whatever, arrives next without the context.</p>

The thread back to the code matters here too. I reference the relevant ADR in the commit message, so anyone running `git blame` over a puzzling change lands on the reasoning behind it rather than just the change itself.

## How it drops out of a session

In practice the record comes early. The decision crystallises in conversation as we uncover and weigh the options, and I have the agent write the ADR before any real code exists, as a *proposed* record rather than a finished one. Writing it before the code is the point of the exercise, because committing the options to a document forces the trade-offs into the open where they can be argued with, before anyone has sunk effort into a particular path. The record leads the implementation rather than reporting on it.

When the code does follow, I like the ADR to ride along in the same commit as the work it justifies, so the reasoning and the change arrive together and the `git blame` trail lines up cleanly. That bends to circumstance. If implementing the decision would mean a long-running branch, the record goes in first, sometimes with the opening steps of the work behind it, rather than waiting weeks to be committed. The branching model shapes the rest, so on an Openfire-style flow the ADR might sit as one commit on a feature branch and reach `main` in the eventual merge alongside everything else, whereas on a more incremental project it lands on its own. The constant through all of it is the intent to keep the trail aligned, so the why and the what are never far apart.

## Why these stay put

There is one discipline I hold for ADRs that I hold for almost nothing else I write with an agent, which is that once a record is merged, it is never rewritten. If a later decision overturns an earlier one, rather than editing the old record into agreement I write a new ADR that supersedes it and mark the original as superseded, so the change of mind is itself part of the history. The point is to show the architectural journey honestly, including the turns that were later reversed, and a chain of superseding records is useful precisely because it reveals which questions a project keeps having to revisit.

That runs directly counter to how I treat working drafts, where I have a rule I call Accuracy Over Archaeology that says to rewrite superseded text in place and leave no trace, so a fresh reader cannot tell the conclusions ever wandered. The two are not in tension once the dividing line is clear, which is the gravity of the document and whether it has been committed. A draft sitting in my working tree is still soft, and tidying it leaves a cleaner artefact. An ADR firms up the instant it lands on `main` and is pushed to a shared remote, and from that moment it is part of the record rather than a thing in progress, so it stops being mine to quietly correct. I lean on the same discipline in [review-loop](https://github.com/viv/review-loop/tree/main/docs/adr), a small reviewing tool of mine, where the choice to use [MCP as its agent integration layer](https://github.com/viv/review-loop/blob/main/docs/adr/ADR-004-mcp-for-agent-integration.md) is a settled foundation the rest of the tool builds on, dependable precisely because it will not be silently rewritten underneath me.

None of this makes an ADR special among the artefacts in this series. A session produces working code, and it produces durable things that make the next session better, and an architecture decision record is one of those, sitting alongside the skills and the scripts and the instruction files. The reason this one is worth its own note is that the durable thing it preserves is a decision, and decisions have a way of being questioned long after everyone has forgotten the conversation that settled them.
