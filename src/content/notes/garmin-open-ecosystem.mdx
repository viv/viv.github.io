## Something I Built Just Broke

A couple of weeks ago I wrote about [wiring an IKEA smart button to log hydration data to Garmin Connect](/notes/one-button-hydration-tracking) via Home Assistant. The integration didn't support writing hydration data, so I added the feature and opened a pull request upstream. It worked beautifully. Press a button, log a pint of water, see it in Garmin's own charts.

Then, around 17 March 2026, Garmin overhauled their authentication infrastructure and broke every third-party integration that relied on it.

## What Happened

The community libraries that make Garmin Connect accessible ([garth](https://github.com/matin/garth), [python-garminconnect](https://github.com/cyberjunky/python-garminconnect), and everything built on top of them) all stopped working overnight. Garmin migrated to a new architecture with aggressive Cloudflare protection with TLS fingerprinting to detect non-browser clients, IP reputation blocking that rejects requests from cloud providers entirely, and rate limiting that returns 429 errors even on a single login attempt from a residential connection.

The maintainers responded quickly. Within days, `garth` shipped a dozen point releases trying to adapt. The `python-garminconnect` maintainer started building an entirely new authentication flow based on JWT tokens extracted from Garmin's revamped web app. People across [both](https://github.com/matin/garth/issues/217) [issue](https://github.com/cyberjunky/python-garminconnect/issues/332) threads reported the same thing from Italy, the US, Sweden, Germany, Australia. This was a deliberate infrastructure change, not a regional outage.

The workarounds indicate how much the community values and wants to retain this access. People are automating real browser sessions with Playwright to extract tokens, generating credentials on residential IPs and copying them to servers, running self-hosted GitHub Actions runners to avoid datacenter IP blocks. The intent isn't to abuse the platform, they just genuinely want to use it.

## A Pattern of Closing Doors

This isn't an isolated incident. In mid-2025, Garmin introduced new API guidelines requiring any platform displaying Garmin-sourced data to include device attribution. The requirement itself was reasonable enough (a small text label crediting the device), but the enforcement mechanism was blunt: comply by November 2025 or lose API access entirely.

Strava, Garmin's biggest integration partner, initially responded by [suing Garmin over patent infringement](https://www.dcrainmaker.com/2025/10/strava-sues-garmin-demands-stop-selling-devices.html) related to segments and heatmaps. They [dropped the lawsuit](https://www.dcrainmaker.com/2025/10/strava-drops-voluntarily-lawsuit-against.html) three weeks later and quietly complied with the attribution rules before the deadline. Whatever was happening behind closed doors, the public sequence tells a clear story of Garmin asserting tighter control over how its data flows through the ecosystem.

What are the options for developers who aren't Strava? Garmin's official Developer Program costs $1,000 USD and is restricted to closed-source projects. At the time of writing, the sign-up page says "Under construction".

## Whose Data Is It?

My watch is on my wrist right now, it's there every day. The data it collects (my heart rate, my steps, my sleep, my hydration) is generated by my body, through my activity, recorded on hardware I paid for. Garmin stores it on their servers and presents it back to me through their app. That's fine. But the idea that I can't programmatically access my own health data because the platform has decided to block non-browser HTTP clients feels fundamentally wrong.

There's a difference between protecting a service from abuse and preventing your customers from using their own data creatively. The community integrations that just broke weren't scraping proprietary algorithms or reverse-engineering competitive features. They were reading and writing personal health metrics. My hydration logs, my activity history.

## The Hardware Earns the Loyalty

It hurts because I genuinely like Garmin's hardware. My Fenix has been on my wrist for years and it's brilliant. The build quality, the battery life, and the sensor accuracy all earn my loyalty in the same way Apple products do. When something works that well, the natural instinct is to invest in the ecosystem around it.

That's exactly what the community has been doing. The Home Assistant Garmin integration, python-garminconnect, garth, and the dozens of projects built on top of them represent thousands of hours of unpaid work by people who love the platform enough to extend it. Every one of those integrations makes Garmin hardware more useful, more embedded in daily life, more worth recommending.

When someone builds a hydration logging integration or a sleep analysis dashboard or an automated training load monitor, they're demonstrating demand for features that Garmin's product team might never have prioritised. They're testing ideas at no cost to Garmin, with real users, generating real feedback.

Garmin recently launched [Connect+](https://www.garmin.com/en-US/p/1565777/), a $6.99/month subscription tier with AI-powered insights, coaching, and performance dashboards. That's a legitimate revenue stream worth protecting. But community developers building open source integrations aren't a threat to it. The vast majority of Garmin users have no interest in writing YAML automations or Python scripts. They'll happily pay for polished AI coaching features. The people tinkering with APIs are a different audience entirely, and a non-commercial, open-source API tier would serve them without cannibalising a single subscription. Nutrition logging is a good example. Connect+ could offer something genuinely useful there, and I'd be tempted to pay for it. But if there's no API access, the data only flows one way. At that point I may as well keep logging to Google Calendar, where I can actually do something with it.

## What Openness Looks Like

Home Assistant is the obvious example of what happens when a platform genuinely welcomes community development. It became the de facto standard for home automation precisely because anyone can build on it. The hardware manufacturers who integrate well with Home Assistant (like IKEA with their Matter-over-Thread devices) sell more hardware as a result.

Fitbit's early API openness is another instructive case. Before Google's acquisition tightened things up, Fitbit had a thriving developer ecosystem that made their relatively basic hardware punch well above its weight. People bought Fitbits partly because of what the community had built around them.

Garmin's hardware doesn't need protecting. It's excellent, and people already recommend it to each other. What would make the ecosystem genuinely stronger is a straightforward, accessible open API that lets people build on top of it. Not a $1,000 closed-source programme. Not an authentication system designed to reject anything that isn't a web browser. Just a stable, documented way for customers to access their own data.

## Where This Leaves Me

I'm not switching hardware yet. The Fenix is good, and while I've flirted with the Apple Watch Ultra I can't see that anything else on the market matches the Fenix for what I need. But the fondness I had for Garmin as a company (not just a product) is harder to sustain when the experience of building on their platform is one of doors closing.

The hydration tracking integration I contributed is broken for me. I'm sure the maintainers will find another workaround, because that's what open source communities do. It shouldn't need to be a workaround. A stable, documented API would make all of this straightforward.
